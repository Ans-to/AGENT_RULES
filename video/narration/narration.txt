Why does the world rely on the U.S. dollar? It wasn't decided by one law. It grew into a system.

In nineteen forty-four, countries met at Bretton Woods. They tied their currencies to the dollar, while foreign central banks could exchange dollars for U.S. gold.

In nineteen seventy-one, the U.S. closed the gold window. The link broke—but the dollar's role survived.

Today, the large U.S. economy and deep Treasury market give investors a place to hold and trade dollar assets.

Businesses invoice trade in dollars. Banks lend in dollars. Central banks hold dollar reserves.

Each use reinforces the others. The more people accept dollars, the more useful dollars become.

But dominance isn't permanent. Other currencies compete, and confidence in any system can change.

The dollar leads not because it's the world's only currency, but because history, markets, and habit keep connecting the world to it.
